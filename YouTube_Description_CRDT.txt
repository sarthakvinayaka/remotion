TITLE:
How Notion Merges Edits Without Conflicts — CRDTs in 10 Lines of Python

DESCRIPTION:

Imagine editing the same Notion page as a co-worker, both of you offline for a few seconds at a time. When your internet comes back, your edits just merge — no popup asking which version to keep, no conflict. That's not luck. That's a CRDT (Conflict-free Replicated Data Type), and the simplest version of one is about 10 lines of Python.

In this video I build a G-Counter — the simplest CRDT there is — from scratch, live, in real Python that actually runs. You'll see exactly why a normal shared counter silently loses updates when two servers change it at the same time, and the one-line trick (taking the max, not the sum) that makes CRDTs mathematically guaranteed to converge no matter what order updates arrive in.

You'll learn:
✅ What a CRDT actually is, in plain terms (no distributed-systems jargon)
✅ Why a normal counter breaks under concurrent updates — and how the bug hides in plain sight
✅ How to build a G-Counter: each node tracks its own slot, increments only touch your own slot
✅ Why merging takes the max per node (not the sum) — and why that's the whole trick
✅ A real live run: two counters updating independently, then merging back to the same final answer
✅ Where CRDTs are actually used in production: Notion, Linear, Yjs, Automerge, Riak, and Redis counters
✅ The one thing Google Docs does NOT use (a common interview gotcha) and how it differs from CRDTs
✅ The limitation of a plain counter (it can only go up) and how a PN-Counter fixes that

This isn't a whiteboard explanation — it's the actual code, actually running, so you can see precisely what "eventual consistency" and "conflict-free merge" really mean underneath the buzzwords.
